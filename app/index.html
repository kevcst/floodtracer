How long does the water stay?

Where did possible flooding appear, and how long did it remain? We compare NISAR radar observations before and after the August storm in six areas of Uruguay. Radar can observe through clouds. Here you can read the results, compare them with other sources and understand their limits.

The event

Where did the storm affect Uruguay?

We study six areas in two river systems: the Laguna Merín basin in the east, and the Yí–Río Negro system farther west. River levels peaked on different days. The timeline gives context from public reports; the map shows where our study areas are.

Timeline (press and SINAE reports, used only as context)

  1. ~23 JulRío Yí reaches 8.95 m in Durazno; 57 people leave their homes.
  2. 27 Jul – 10 AugNo NISAR observations during this gap. June–July observations provide the before-storm reference.
  3. 15–19 AugINUMET warning: 70–100 mm/day, over 250 mm in five eastern departments; 317 mm at Laguna Merín.
  4. 19–20 AugOlimar crests at ~8.6 m in Treinta y Tres; ~1,600 people displaced in the city.
  5. 22 AugYí crests at 9.75 m in Durazno, three days after the Olimar. National peak: 2,229 displaced.
  6. 23 AugReported displacement falls to 418 people in Treinta y Tres and rises to 416 in Durazno.
  7. ~21–25 AugSoriano prepares for the Río Negro at Mercedes.

What L-band adds

Water under the trees

Water beneath trees is hard to see in ordinary satellite photos. NISAR's longer radar waves can interact with water and tree trunks, changing the returning signal. We compare riverside forest with forest on higher ground to look for this pattern. It is evidence of possible flooding beneath vegetation, not direct confirmation of water.

Method

How does the analysis work?

Data behind the current map

How we identify possible flooding · technical details
  • NISAR L2 GCOV, HH and HV gamma-naught, 20 m, PROVISIONAL products only (June–October 2026). 5×5 speckle filter. NISAR data guide.
  • A map cell is flagged as possible flooding when its HH drops more than 3 dB below its own June–July median, is darker than −14 dB, lies outside permanent water, and belongs to a patch of at least 10 pixels (0.4 ha). Each pass is first normalized by its scene-wide median change.
  • We compare each small map cell with earlier observations from the same satellite path. This reduces differences caused by viewing angle and signal calibration when combining paths.
  • Flood extent combines detections from at least two observations between 15 Aug (start of the rain) and 10 Sep. Single-track areas use at least one detection.
  • Already wet before the storm: NISAR had a global data gap until 10 Aug. Land already wet on the 10–14 Aug passes (from July rains) cannot be attributed to this storm or dated, so it is reported separately and excluded from extent, duration and road figures.
How we estimate duration · technical details
  • We combine the available water maps from different satellite paths. In areas with several paths, this gives observations closer together than the 12-day repeat of a single path.
  • For combined tracks, a pixel has dried at the first dry observation confirmed by the next one, except at the end of the series. Single-track results use the first later dry observation.
  • Satellite visits cannot tell us the exact hour water arrived or left. We calculate a shortest and longest duration from nearby wet and dry observations, starting no earlier than 15 Aug. Maps show the midpoint of that range.
  • If there is no later dry observation, we can only say the water lasted at least this long. The “20 Oct” label is a reporting cutoff; it may differ from the actual observation date.
Forest signal · technical details

Candidate riverside forest is selected using baseline HH/HV and approximate height and distance to streams. The mask flags an HH−HV rise over 2 dB beyond the upland reference, with an HH rise over 1 dB on an event pass. This signal is compatible with possible inundation beneath vegetation; it is not independently confirmed water.

What these results cannot tell us

  • Each satellite path repeats every 12 days. Events between visits can be missed. Duration estimates describe the first detected wet episode, in days rather than hours.
  • These layers estimate water presence and timing; they do not measure water depth, volume, damage or road closures.
  • Cities are under-counted: buildings bounce the radar back like flooded forest does.
  • Data arrives days after each pass: this is post-event assessment and planning, not early warning.
  • We use one provisional product version throughout. Later validated products have different calibration and are kept separate.
  • NISAR flags more area than the strict Sentinel-1 open-water map. Some extra detections may be shallow or partly flooded land; disagreement can also include errors (see Checks).
  • Press and SINAE figures are preliminary and used only as context.

How could this help?

  • Emergency teams: identify places and roads to investigate after a flood, alongside local reports.
  • Farmers and planners: compare the area and estimated duration of detected flooding, even during cloudy weather.
  • Local planning: use this event to identify places where water remained longer. This does not predict the next flood.
  • Other regions: adapt the workflow to available NISAR data, then check it against local conditions and independent observations.
