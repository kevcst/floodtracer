How long does the water stay?

Clouds blinded optical satellites during the August storm. NISAR's L-band radar did not. Pass after pass it mapped where new water appeared, how far it spread, what it cut off and when the land dried again, from the rice fields of the Laguna Merín basin to the forests along the Río Negro.

The event

One storm, two watersheds

The Cuchilla Grande splits Uruguay's east. Rain on the Atlantic side drains through the Olimar, Cebollatí and Yaguarón into the Laguna Merín. Rain on the other side runs down the Yí into the Río Negro. The same storm produced crests days apart. Click an area to explore it.

Timeline (press and SINAE reports, used only as context)

  1. ~23 JulRío Yí reaches 8.95 m in Durazno; 57 people leave their homes.
  2. 27 Jul – 10 AugGlobal NISAR data gap. June–July passes serve as the "normal" baseline.
  3. 15–19 AugINUMET warning: 70–100 mm/day, over 250 mm in five eastern departments; 317 mm at Laguna Merín.
  4. 19–20 AugOlimar crests at ~8.6 m in Treinta y Tres; ~1,600 people displaced in the city.
  5. 22 AugYí crests at 9.75 m in Durazno, three days after the Olimar. National peak: 2,229 displaced.
  6. 23 AugTreinta y Tres drops to 418 displaced while Durazno rises to 416: the flood moves west.
  7. ~21–25 AugSoriano prepares for the Río Negro at Mercedes. El Niño expected to last until at least May 2027.

What L-band adds

Water under the trees

When a forest floor floods, the radar wave bounces between the water surface and the trunks and comes back stronger (HH rises over HV). L-band's 24 cm wave reaches the ground through the canopy; optical satellites and C-band radar mostly can't. Forest on higher ground also brightens a little when everything is wet, so we subtract it: what's left over riverside forest is the flood signal.

3 · River crests

Does the radar peak follow the reported crest?

NISAR revisits each track every 12 days, so it can't pin the crest. What it must do is light up on the first pass after it and fade afterwards, east before west. Peak = pass with most new open water between 10 Aug and 10 Sep; on the Yí and Río Negro open water is weak (the flood stays under the forest), so their peak is less meaningful.

Method

How the maps are made

Detecting new water

  • NISAR L2 GCOV, HH and HV gamma-naught, 20 m, PROVISIONAL products only (June–October 2026). 5×5 speckle filter.
  • A pixel has new water when its HH drops more than 3 dB below its own June–July median, is darker than −14 dB, lies outside permanent water, and belongs to a patch of at least 10 pixels (0.4 ha). Each pass is first normalized by its scene-wide median change.
  • Comparing each pixel with itself cancels the geometry, incidence angle and calibration of each orbit track. That is what let us combine tracks.
  • Flood extent = new water on at least two observations (any track) between 10 Aug and 10 Sep.

Measuring how long it stayed

  • All passes from all tracks over an area are merged at mask level (2–3 tracks, every 1–6 days).
  • A pixel has dried at the first dry observation confirmed by the next one.
  • Days under water is an interval: from the last wet pass minus the first wet pass, up to the first dry pass minus the pass before the water arrived (never before the rain started on 15 Aug). Maps show the midpoint.
  • Pixels still wet on the last pass (20 Oct) are counted with a lower bound.

Limits

  • 12-day revisit per track: crests between passes are missed; persistence has days, not hours, of precision.
  • Cities are under-counted: buildings bounce the radar back like flooded forest does.
  • Data arrives days after each pass: this is post-event assessment and planning, not early warning.
  • PROVISIONAL calibration. VALIDATED products (from 21 Oct) jump ~−1.7 dB and are not mixed in.
  • Extents include shallow and partly flooded land: compared with a Sentinel-1 open-water map they are an upper bound (see Validation).
  • Press and SINAE figures are preliminary and used only as context.

What this enables

  • SINAE / CECOED: which rural roads and neighbourhoods stayed cut off longest, to prioritize repairs and assistance after the next flood.
  • Producers and insurers: hectare-days of flooding per paddock, independent of cloud cover.
  • Planning for El Niño 2026-27: the persistence map is a map of where water lingers. That is where the next flood will hurt longest.
  • Beyond Uruguay: the same pipeline runs on any NISAR GCOV scene. Only the area list changes.
